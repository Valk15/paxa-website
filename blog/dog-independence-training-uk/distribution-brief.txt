ANGLE: General behaviour share — pitched at someone who casually mentioned

------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is something I spent a long time getting wrong, so sharing in case it
helps anyone else in the same situation.

My dog was what I'd describe as a shadow — followed me from room to room,
would start pacing the moment she heard my keys, and if I left for more than
ten minutes she'd either bark continuously or chew whatever was nearest the
door. I assumed it was because she loved me. Turns out that framing was part
of the problem.

What actually shifted things was learning the difference between a dog who
is attached and a dog who genuinely cannot regulate their own emotional state
when alone. Those look identical from the outside but they need completely
different responses. A dog with real separation-related anxiety is
experiencing a stress response — cortisol goes up, the amygdala treats
absence as a threat — and no amount of love or "getting used to it" resolves
that on its own.

The approach that finally worked for us was something called independence
training, which is not the same as crate training and definitely not "tough
love." The core idea is threshold management — you never push the dog past
the point where they start to panic, because a panicked dog is not learning
anything, they are just surviving. Every session is designed to keep them
just below the anxiety threshold so the nervous system can actually start to
associate being alone with something neutral, or eventually positive.

In practical terms this meant absences of 10–15 seconds at first. Genuinely.
Not minutes — seconds. Building duration is a slow process, but it compounds.
By week three we were at 8-minute absences with no reaction at all.

The other thing that made a difference: changing what I did before and after
leaving. Low-key departures, low-key returns. No long goodbyes, no excited
greetings when I got back. It felt cold at first but it removes the emotional
spikes that signal to the dog that your coming and going is a big deal.

There is a lot of noise out there about calming supplements and anxiety
wraps, and I am not saying they are useless, but they do not address the
underlying pattern. The pattern needs systematic work.

the full breakdown: https://paxapet.co.uk/blog/dog-independence-training-uk/

------------------------------------------------------------------------

I went through this with my own dog about eighteen months ago and I want to
give you the honest answer rather than the easy one: calming treats and
leaving the radio on are unlikely to fix this, and here is why.

Separation anxiety — or more precisely, separation-related distress — is a
neurological pattern, not a personality trait. The dog's brain has learned
that being alone equals threat. Cortisol spikes, the amygdala fires, and the
dog goes into a state of genuine stress. Supplements can blunt the edge of
that response but they do not rewrite the underlying association. The only
thing that rewrites it is repeated experience of being alone that does not
result in the feared outcome — i.e. you going and not coming back, or
something bad happening.

The method that is best supported by behavioural science for this is
systematic desensitisation. The principle is straightforward: you expose the
dog to the trigger (you being absent) at a level so low that it does not
produce anxiety. No anxiety means the brain is available to learn. You build
duration incrementally, always staying below the threshold where distress
kicks in.

A few concrete things that helped us:

1. Start with absences of under 30 seconds. I know that sounds absurd but if
   your dog is already anxious, even 2-minute absences are too long. You need
   to find the level where they are calm, and start there.

2. Vary the duration randomly so the dog cannot predict when you are coming
   back. Predictability creates anticipatory anxiety.

3. Do not make a ritual of leaving. The longer your pre-departure routine,
   the more time the dog has to escalate. Keys, coat, out. That is it.

4. The return matters as much as the departure. A calm return — no excited
   greeting, no immediate fuss — signals that your coming and going is
   unremarkable.

Progress is slow and it is not linear. There will be setbacks if you push
too far too fast. But the gains are real and they stick, because you are
actually changing how the dog's nervous system is organised, not just
managing symptoms.

SUBREDDIT: r/puppy101
ANGLE: Prevention — building independence early in young dogs
------------------------------------------------------------------------

One thing I wish someone had told me when I got my dog as a puppy: the
behaviours that look adorable at eight weeks can become a serious problem at
eighteen months. A puppy that follows you everywhere, cries the moment you
leave the room, sleeps on you every night — none of that is inherently bad,
but if you do not also teach them that being alone is safe, you are building
a dog that genuinely cannot cope when you are not there.

The good news with young dogs is that their nervous systems are more plastic.
It is much easier to build a positive association with solitude early than
to undo an entrenched anxiety response later.

Independence training for puppies is less about fixing a problem and more
about proactively teaching a skill. The core principle is threshold
management — you keep alone time short enough that the puppy stays calm,
and you build duration gradually. Even 20–30 seconds of calm alone time,
repeated consistently, starts to wire in the idea that being alone is a
normal, unremarkable part of life.

A few things worth doing from the start:

- Practice micro-separations from week one. Go out of sight for 10 seconds
  and come back. Do this many times a day.
- Do not reward the puppy for following you. Following is fine, but teaching
  a settle in a separate area — even briefly — is worth the effort.
- Make departures and returns low-key from the beginning. If your puppy
  learns that you leaving is a massive emotional event, they will treat it as
  one.
- Give them something positive during alone time (a stuffed Kong, a chew)
  so their brain starts to associate your absence with good things rather
  than nothing or distress.

Counter-conditioning and systematic desensitisation are the terms you will
see in the literature. The concepts are not complicated but the application
requires patience and a willingness to go slower than feels necessary.

adult dogs with existing anxiety but the underlying approach is the same and
works just as well as a preventive framework for puppies:

Anyone working on helping their dog cope better alone — worth knowing about
the one thing that quietly undoes progress.

It is not leaving for too long. It is the return. Coming home and immediately
giving your dog a lot of fuss — even calmly — signals that your absence was
a significant event, which reinforces the anxiety cycle rather than
interrupting it.
